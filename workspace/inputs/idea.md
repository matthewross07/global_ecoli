## Problem Statement

Fecal indicator bacteria (FIB), and *Escherichia coli* in particular, are the
primary tool used worldwide to judge whether inland fresh waters — rivers,
lakes, and reservoirs used for recreation and drinking-water supply — are safe
from waterborne pathogens. Yet the global monitoring record that underpins
these safety decisions has never been assembled and characterized at a global,
harmonized scale. Without that synthesis, two structural weaknesses in the
monitoring enterprise remain invisible and unquantified: where freshwater FIB
data are actually collected and shared, and whether the cadence of sampling is
fast enough to protect public health.

## Core Hypothesis

We hypothesize that the global freshwater FIB monitoring record is failing
public health along two distinct and measurable axes simultaneously:

1. **Spatial sparsity and an access/sharing barrier.** Publicly available,
   harmonized freshwater FIB data are concentrated in a handful of wealthy
   regions. The apparent emptiness elsewhere reflects not only a true
   collection gap in resource-limited nations but also a *sharing gap* — major
   monitoring nations whose grab-sample data are simply not published in open,
   machine-readable, harmonized repositories.

2. **Temporal mismatch ("regulatory rigidity").** Fecal contamination is a
   transient, weather-driven condition that rises and decays within days, but
   the dominant sampling designs are weekly, monthly, or quarterly and are
   often confined to a summer bathing season. The data are therefore abundant
   for compliance reporting yet rarely actionable for real-time safety.

## Proposed Methodology (High-Level Technical Approach)

We propose to compile, harmonize, and analyze a global freshwater FIB database
by aggregating every openly available point-observation network we can obtain,
standardizing each to a common schema, unit, and indicator vocabulary, and
restricting the result to inland freshwaters. The approach has four conceptual
stages: (a) retrieve from national/international portals and curated releases;
(b) harmonize units, censoring, location types, and de-duplicate within and
across sources; (c) clean to a canonical FIB keep-list and classify each record
as freshwater, marine, or groundwater so that marine bathing waters are removed
by design; and (d) characterize the resulting record geographically, by
indicator, by ecosystem, and — most importantly — by sampling cadence, using
per-site return intervals and the behavior of the rare high-frequency sites as
a window onto what actionable monitoring would reveal.

## Expected Contribution

The contribution is twofold. First, a harmonized, openly described global
freshwater FIB dataset that did not previously exist, with a transparent
provenance and known-limitations record. Second, a quantitative
characterization that reframes "data sparsity" as substantially an open-data
access and sharing problem, and that demonstrates the public-health cost of
rigid, low-frequency sampling — motivating a shift toward event-based and
predictive monitoring and toward international data-sharing norms analogous to
those in global meteorology.
